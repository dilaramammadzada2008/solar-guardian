# Solar Guardian · speaker notes

Seven slides. Read the notes aloud; the live demo goes after slide 5.

## 1. Solar Guardian

Hello, we are Solar Guardian. Our challenge is health monitoring software for astronauts. The question the mentors asked us was: who is it for? Our answer: the astronaut, who is not a doctor. Solar Guardian is a simple app with five tabs and one emergency button, built on what NASA already flies and plans. In the next minutes we show why that matters on the way to Mars.

## 2. Halfway to Mars, a crewmate collapses

The challenge video starts with this scene: halfway to Mars, a crewmate feels chest pain and collapses. What do you do? On the ISS today the answer is simple: a flight surgeon on Earth talks to the crew within a second. That is how real cases were handled: a blood clot found in 2020 was treated from Earth for more than 90 days, and the Crew-11 crew managed a medical event in January 2026 with doctors guiding them. On Mars this does not work. Our app reads today's real Earth-Mars light time from NASA's Deep Space Network: almost 11 minutes one way. A question and its answer take 22 minutes. And the person helping is not a doctor.

## 3. The sensor sees what changed. The astronaut tells us why.

This is the core idea of Solar Guardian. On a spacecraft, devices already measure a lot: the smart shirt, the sleep sensor, the cabin air system. Our app measures nothing itself; it reads them. What sensors can never know is why something changed. Example: at 13:10 the heart rate rose by 24 beats for 40 minutes, and nothing on the crew schedule explains it. Instead of an alarm, the app asks the astronaut one question: what was happening? One tap: physical work, stress, coffee, or felt unwell. The answer decides what happens. Coffee: no action. Stress twice in one day: a follow-up. Felt unwell: see the medical officer today. The rule behind each decision is always shown, and the note goes to the medical officer, so the doctor gets the numbers together with the reason. It is built for the astronaut, who is not a doctor: plain words, one question at a time.

## 4. Five tabs, one task each

Mentors told us the first version was one long page and too complex for an astronaut. So the astronaut's screen now has five tabs, each for one task, while the status line and the emergency button stay on top all the time. Today is the main tab: four plain-word cards and one question at a time. When the heart rate rose and nothing on the schedule explains it, the app asks what was happening, and the answer decides: no action, a follow-up, or see the medical officer today, with buttons for what to do next. My body shows the 3D body coloured by today's data. Log and messages mirrors the real ISS routine: food and water log, exercise minutes, and a private message to the flight surgeon or psychologist that never goes into the crew report. Health items and report holds the follow-ups and one structured report for the medical officer and Earth. Data and sources shows which onboard device each value comes from and the NASA documents behind our rules.

## 5. Step by step on board, with a clear ending

This is the challenge scene: a crewmate collapses and the person next to them is not a doctor. One button opens the emergency screen and sends an SOS. It asks: can the person answer you? If not, the first step is to shout for help and send someone for the defibrillator. Then check breathing for no more than 10 seconds, and start chest compressions with a beat of 110 per minute; trained crew add 2 breaths after every 30. It explains CPR in weightlessness: alone, the Evetts-Russomano method, developed by a Brazilian researcher; once the patient is strapped down, the handstand method used in the ISS protocol. The moment the button is pressed, an SOS goes to the medical officer and the Commander, and a message to the flight surgeon on Earth is queued. When the medical officer arrives, the astronaut taps "CMO is here". The emergency always ends clearly: the crew member presses how it ended, for example "breathing again", and gets a timed summary of what happened and what happens next, including when the flight surgeon on Earth will see it, about 11 minutes from Mars today.

## 6. Built on what NASA already flies and plans

The mentors told us NASA already has systems, so integrate. We did. Our design follows NASA's own concept for an onboard clinical decision support system and the Earth-Independent Medical Operations plan: crew members log symptoms themselves, follow-up uses improved, no change or worsened, and when several crew are affected the system checks the cabin first. Every health item gets a NASA Integrated Medical Model category, and the report is machine-readable for an assistant like NASA and Google's CMO-DA. Our app measures nothing itself: it reads from devices that fly, like the Canadian Bio-Monitor shirt, the Artemis II wrist actigraph made in Brazil, the personal CO2 monitor, and the live delay from NASA's Deep Space Network. We also kept the real ISS medical routine: the weekly private medical conference becomes a private message, because a live call from Mars is impossible; the 2.5 hours of daily exercise on ARED, the T2 treadmill and the CEVIS bike; and the daily food log that goes to the dietitians every week. On Earth, the same approach helps people far from a doctor.

## 7. What is real, what comes next, and how we used SI

We want to be honest about what is real. The Earth–Mars delay is live NASA data, the SOS is sent the moment the emergency button is pressed, and every rule is visible. The health data, the crew and the day are simulated, the limits are general adult values, and this is not a medical device. Next, we would read real data from the Bio-Monitor shirt and the actigraph, let flight surgeons set personal limits, and test in analog missions like HERA and CHAPEA. As the Space Apps rules require, here is how we used Super Intelligence tools. We used Claude from Anthropic to write and debug code, to find and summarise NASA sources, and to draft the English text and slides. The team came up with the idea, made the design decisions based on mentor feedback, checked every source ourselves and tested the app by hand. There is no SI inside the app: the decisions come from simple rules that are shown to the astronaut. Thank you, we are happy to take questions.
